PHYSIOTHERAPY CLINICAL REFERENCE GUIDE
Evidence-Based Assessment and Treatment of Musculoskeletal Pain
======================================================================

CHAPTER 1: LUMBAR SPINE (LOWER BACK)
======================================================================

Anatomy Overview
----------------
The lumbar spine consists of five vertebrae (L1-L5) supported by the erector spinae, multifidus, quadratus lumborum, and deep stabilizing muscles including the transverse abdominis. The lumbar region bears the majority of axial compressive load and is the most common site of musculoskeletal pain in adults.

Common Pain Mechanisms
-----------------------
1. Muscular Deconditioning and Postural Overload
Prolonged sitting or static postures create sustained eccentric load on the lumbar erector spinae and multifidus, resulting in muscle fatigue, microtrauma, and ischemic pain. This is the most prevalent mechanism in sedentary populations and desk workers. The pain is typically dull, aching, and localised to the central or bilateral paraspinal region.

2. Facet Joint Dysfunction
Repetitive extension or rotational loading can irritate the zygapophyseal joints. Clinically, pain is unilateral, worsens with extension and lateral flexion, and is relieved by forward flexion. Morning stiffness is common due to joint capsule tightening during recumbency.

3. Lumbar Disc Pathology
Nucleus pulposus herniation typically occurs at L4-L5 or L5-S1. Non-specific low back pain from disc degeneration presents as central aching without distal radiation. The McKenzie method of repeated end-range extension is evidence-based for centralisation of discogenic pain.

4. Segmental Instability
Hypermobility of a spinal segment due to weakened deep stabilizers (multifidus, transverse abdominis) causes recurrent pain episodes with provocative loading.

Red Flags (Refer Immediately)
-------------------------------
- Saddle anaesthesia or bilateral lower limb weakness (cauda equina)
- Fever with back pain (infective pathology, spinal abscess)
- Unremitting night pain, unexplained weight loss (malignancy)
- History of significant trauma, osteoporosis risk factors
- Bowel or bladder dysfunction

Evidence-Based Treatment Approaches
-------------------------------------
First Line:
- Graded activity and avoidance of bed rest (NICE guidelines)
- Motor control exercise targeting the multifidus and transverse abdominis (activation, isometric holds, progressing to dynamic loading)
- McKenzie mechanical diagnosis and therapy for directional preference patients
- Cognitive behavioural approaches for chronic presentations

Stretching and Mobility:
- Knee-to-chest stretch: decompresses lumbar facet joints
- Child's pose (if extension is not the directional preference): gentle lumbar flexion
- Hip flexor stretching: reduces anterior pelvic tilt and lumbar lordosis
- Cat-cow mobilisation: rhythmic flexion-extension to restore segmental mobility

Strengthening:
- Dead bug exercise: low-load transverse abdominis activation with limb movement
- Bird-dog: simultaneous contralateral arm-leg extension for multifidus recruitment
- Glute bridge: posterior chain activation to unload lumbar extensors
- Pallof press progression: anti-rotation core stability

CHAPTER 2: TRAPEZIUS AND UPPER BACK (TRAPEZIUS SPINE)
======================================================================

Anatomy Overview
----------------
The trapezius is a large, flat muscle spanning from the occipital protuberance to the thoracic spine and lateral clavicle. It is functionally divided into upper (elevation, upward rotation of scapula), middle (retraction), and lower (depression, upward rotation) fibres. The upper trapezius is disproportionately overactivated in desk workers, leading to hypertonicity and trigger point development.

Common Pain Mechanisms
-----------------------
1. Upper Crossed Syndrome
A postural dysfunction characterised by tight upper trapezius and levator scapulae combined with inhibited deep neck flexors and lower trapezius. Results in forward head posture, protracted shoulders, and chronically elevated upper trapezius tone. Highly prevalent in computer users.

2. Myofascial Trigger Points
Active trigger points within the upper trapezius refer pain to the lateral neck, temporal region, and occiput. Sustained static contraction during keyboard and mouse use is the primary precipitant.

3. Scapular Dyskinesis
Altered scapular kinematics due to lower and middle trapezius weakness creates compensatory overactivity of the upper trapezius during arm elevation. This leads to shoulder impingement secondary to reduced subacromial space.

Red Flags
----------
- Sudden severe headache with neck rigidity (meningism)
- Progressive neurological deficit in upper limb
- Cervical spine fracture risk in trauma patients

Evidence-Based Treatment Approaches
-------------------------------------
Trigger Point Release:
- Ischaemic compression: sustained digital pressure on active trigger point for 60-90 seconds
- Dry needling (clinician-administered): most effective for active trigger points
- Foam roller thoracic extension over a foam roller improves thoracic kyphosis and reduces upper trapezius compensatory activity

Stretching:
- Upper trapezius stretch: lateral cervical flexion with contralateral arm depression
- Levator scapulae stretch: cervical flexion with ipsilateral rotation
- Doorway chest stretch: restores pectoralis minor length to allow scapular retraction

Strengthening:
- Lower trapezius activation: prone Y raises, wall slides
- Serratus anterior activation: wall push-up plus, protraction-retraction sequences
- Deep neck flexor strengthening (chin tuck): counteracts forward head posture

CHAPTER 3: HAMSTRINGS
======================================================================

Anatomy Overview
----------------
The hamstring group consists of biceps femoris (long and short heads), semimembranosus, and semitendinosus. These biarticular muscles cross both the hip and knee, creating complex length-tension relationships. They function as hip extensors and knee flexors and are primary decelerators during the terminal swing phase of gait.

Common Pain Mechanisms
-----------------------
1. Acute Strain (Grades I-III)
Most common in sprinting and explosive activities. The proximal musculotendinous junction (biceps femoris long head) is the most vulnerable site. Mechanism: eccentric overload during terminal swing when the hamstring decelerates hip flexion and begins knee extension simultaneously.

2. Chronic Tightness and Mobility Deficit
Sustained hip flexion in seated populations shortens the hamstring beyond its optimal operating length. This reduces neural extensibility and may compress the sciatic nerve at the piriformis or biceps femoris tunnel.

3. Proximal Hamstring Tendinopathy
Compressive loading of the proximal tendon during sustained hip flexion (sitting on a hard surface, cycling) leads to reactive tendinopathy. Pain is localised to the ischial tuberosity.

Evidence-Based Treatment Approaches
-------------------------------------
Acute Strain:
- POLICE protocol: Protect, Optimal Loading, Ice, Compression, Elevation
- Avoid aggressive static stretching in the first 72 hours
- Progressive eccentric loading introduced at 2-4 weeks

Flexibility and Mobility:
- Static hamstring stretch: supine with hip at 90°, knee extension to tolerance (hold 30-45 seconds)
- Proprioceptive Neuromuscular Facilitation (PNF): contract-relax technique achieves superior gains vs. static stretching
- Standing hamstring stretch with neural component: dorsiflexion added to bias sciatic nerve mobilisation

Strengthening and Rehabilitation:
- Nordic hamstring curl: most evidence-supported eccentric exercise for hamstring injury prevention. Reduces re-injury risk by up to 70% (Petersen et al., 2011)
- Romanian deadlift: progresses load through the full eccentric range
- Hamstring curl in long sitting position: targets medial hamstrings specifically

CHAPTER 4: QUADRICEPS
======================================================================

Anatomy Overview
----------------
The quadriceps femoris comprises the rectus femoris, vastus lateralis, vastus medialis (including VMO), and vastus intermedius. As the primary knee extensor and hip flexor (rectus femoris), it plays a crucial role in stair climbing, squatting, and decelerating during landing tasks.

Common Pain Mechanisms
-----------------------
1. Patellofemoral Pain Syndrome (PFPS)
Altered patellar tracking due to VMO inhibition, tight iliotibial band, or increased Q-angle causes repetitive stress on the patellofemoral articular surface. Pain is anterior knee, aggravated by prolonged sitting (cinema sign), stair descent, and squatting.

2. Quadriceps Tendinopathy
Reactive tendinopathy at the inferior pole of the patella from repetitive jumping and landing (jumper's knee). Load management and isometric quadriceps exercise are first-line treatment.

3. Post-Surgical Inhibition
Quadriceps atrophy following ACL reconstruction or total knee arthroplasty results from arthrogenic muscle inhibition, a reflexive mechanism mediated by joint effusion. Neuromuscular electrical stimulation (NMES) augments voluntary contraction in this context.

Evidence-Based Treatment Approaches
-------------------------------------
VMO Activation:
- Terminal knee extension with resistance band: high VMO:VL ratio
- Single-leg press at shallow range: reduces patellofemoral joint reaction force
- Step-up and step-down: functional VMO recruitment in closed chain

Stretching:
- Standing rectus femoris stretch: hip extension while maintaining neutral lumbar spine
- Side-lying quadriceps stretch: targets VL and IT band concurrently
- Prone quad stretch: adds hip extension component for rectus femoris bias

Strengthening Progression:
- Isometric quad set → short arc quad → wall squat → full squat with load

CHAPTER 5: GLUTEAL MUSCLES (GLUTES)
======================================================================

Anatomy Overview
----------------
The gluteal complex includes gluteus maximus (primary hip extensor), gluteus medius (primary hip abductor and pelvic stabiliser), and gluteus minimus. The piriformis, obturators, and gemelli form the deep external rotators. Gluteal insufficiency is a significant contributing factor in lumbopelvic, hip, knee, and ankle pain syndromes.

Common Pain Mechanisms
-----------------------
1. Gluteus Medius Weakness and Trendelenburg Gait
Inhibited gluteus medius during the stance phase of gait allows contralateral pelvic drop, increasing compressive and shear forces at the ipsilateral hip, SI joint, IT band, and knee.

2. Piriformis Syndrome
Hypertonic piriformis can compress the sciatic nerve, causing buttock pain with referral into the posterior thigh. Differentiated from true lumbar disc pathology by the absence of spinal signs (no nerve root tension test positivity at the spine).

3. Gluteal Tendinopathy
Compressive loading of the gluteus medius and minimus tendons at the greater trochanter during adduction (cross-leg sitting, sleeping with hips adducted) leads to trochanteric bursitis or tendinopathy.

Evidence-Based Treatment Approaches
-------------------------------------
Activation:
- Clamshell exercise: gluteus medius isolation in side-lying hip abduction
- Side-lying hip abduction: pure abductor strengthening
- Supine bridge: bilateral gluteus maximus activation

Strengthening:
- Single-leg bridge: introduces pelvic stability challenge
- Side-stepping with resistance band: functional gluteus medius loading
- Hip thrust: peak gluteus maximus EMG activation; 25-35° hip flexion target angle
- Step-up: functional closed-chain loading in all gluteal components

Piriformis Stretching:
- Supine piriformis stretch (figure-4): hip flexion, external rotation, adduction
- Seated piriformis stretch: cross-legged forward lean

CHAPTER 6: CERVICAL SPINE (NECK)
======================================================================

Anatomy Overview
----------------
The cervical spine is the most mobile region of the spinal column, with seven vertebrae (C1-C7). The upper cervical spine (occiput-C2) controls approximately 50% of cervical rotation. The lower cervical spine is the most common site of disc degeneration and facet joint pathology. The deep cervical flexors (longus colli, longus capitis) are the primary stabilisers.

Common Pain Mechanisms
-----------------------
1. Cervicogenic Headache
Referral of upper cervical nociception (C1-C3 afferents converge with trigeminal nucleus) produces occipital or retro-orbital headache. Provoked by sustained flexion postures.

2. Cervical Facet Dysfunction
Most common at C4-C5, C5-C6. Unilateral neck pain worse with end-range rotation and extension. Managed with manual therapy and deep neck flexor training.

3. Forward Head Posture (Anterior Head Displacement)
Each centimetre of anterior displacement from neutral increases cervical compressive load by approximately 4.5 kg. Causes chronic upper trapezius and suboccipital overload.

Evidence-Based Treatment Approaches
-------------------------------------
Deep Neck Flexor Training:
- Cranio-cervical flexion test (CCFT): diagnostic and therapeutic at 22-28 mmHg progressive pressure biofeedback levels
- Chin tuck (cervical retraction): restores neutral cervical alignment, activates longus colli
- Chin tuck with nodding: progressive deep flexor endurance

Mobilisation and Stretching:
- Cervical lateral flexion stretch: upper trapezius and scalene lengthening
- Upper cervical rotation mobilisation: restores C1-C2 rotation in cervicogenic headache
- Thoracic extension over foam roller: reduces compensatory cervical hyperextension

CHAPTER 7: CALVES (GASTROCNEMIUS AND SOLEUS)
======================================================================

Anatomy Overview
----------------
The calf complex consists of the gastrocnemius (biarticular, crosses knee and ankle) and soleus (uniarticular, ankle only). Both converge into the Achilles tendon. The calf musculature is the primary plantarflexor and contributes significantly to propulsion during gait. It also serves as a venous pump for lower limb blood return.

Common Pain Mechanisms
-----------------------
1. Gastrocnemius Strain
Occurs at the medial gastrocnemius musculotendinous junction during push-off in explosive activities. Known as "tennis leg." Acute, sharp pain with ecchymosis and palpable defect in grade III.

2. Achilles Tendinopathy
Insertional (at calcaneal insertion) or mid-portion (2-6 cm proximal). Compressive mechanism at insertional type requires avoidance of stretching into dorsiflexion. Mid-portion responds to eccentric heel drop protocol (Alfredson et al.).

3. Soleus Tightness
Isolated soleus tightness (assessed with knee flexed) restricts ankle dorsiflexion, creating compensatory pronation and proximal chain dysfunction (knee valgus, hip internal rotation).

Evidence-Based Treatment Approaches
-------------------------------------
Eccentric Loading:
- Eccentric heel drop (Alfredson protocol): 3 sets of 15 repetitions, twice daily, with knee straight (gastrocnemius) and knee bent (soleus). Performed over 12 weeks.
- Heavy slow resistance calf raise: preferred for insertional tendinopathy

Stretching:
- Standing gastrocnemius stretch: knee extended, foot dorsiflexed against wall
- Standing soleus stretch: knee flexed, foot dorsiflexed
- Seated towel stretch: gentle morning stretch before weight-bearing

CHAPTER 8: SHOULDER
======================================================================

Anatomy Overview
----------------
The glenohumeral joint relies on the dynamic stabilisers of the rotator cuff (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis) and the static constraints of the glenoid labrum and capsuloligamentous complex. The scapulothoracic joint contributes significantly to overhead mobility through upward scapular rotation.

Common Pain Mechanisms
-----------------------
1. Rotator Cuff Tendinopathy and Impingement
Supraspinatus tendinopathy is the most common shoulder condition. Compression of the supraspinatus tendon between the humeral head and coracoacromial arch during arm elevation (classic painful arc 60-120°). Lower trapezius and serratus anterior weakness disrupts scapular upward rotation, reducing subacromial space.

2. Adhesive Capsulitis (Frozen Shoulder)
Progressive capsular contracture leads to global range of motion restriction in a capsular pattern (external rotation > abduction > internal rotation). Idiopathic or post-immobilisation.

3. Acromioclavicular Joint Pathology
Horizontal adduction pain at the AC joint. Positive cross-body adduction test.

Evidence-Based Treatment Approaches
-------------------------------------
Rotator Cuff Rehabilitation:
- Side-lying external rotation: isolated infraspinatus and teres minor exercise
- Prone shoulder abduction (scaption): lower trapezius and supraspinatus activation
- Cable or band external rotation: progressive loading through range

Scapular Stabilisation:
- Scapular retraction and depression: lower and middle trapezius recruitment
- Wall slides: progressive serratus anterior and lower trapezius co-activation
- Push-up plus: end-range serratus anterior loading

Frozen Shoulder Mobilisation:
- Pendulum exercises: distraction-assisted glenohumeral mobilisation
- Posterior capsule stretch: horizontal adduction in side-lying (sleeper stretch)
- Rope and pulley assisted flexion

CHAPTER 9: HIP FLEXORS
======================================================================

Anatomy Overview
----------------
The hip flexor complex includes the iliopsoas (iliacus + psoas major), rectus femoris, tensor fascia latae, sartorius, and pectineus. The psoas major is the only muscle directly connecting the lumbar spine to the lower extremity, making its length a critical determinant of lumbar lordosis and anterior pelvic tilt.

Common Pain Mechanisms
-----------------------
1. Hip Flexor Tightness (Postural and Adaptive Shortening)
Sustained hip flexion during prolonged sitting adaptively shortens the iliopsoas and rectus femoris. This anterior pelvic tilt and lumbar hyperlordosis loads the posterior lumbar facet joints and increases compressive forces on the lumbar disc.

2. Iliopsoas Bursitis and Tendinopathy
Internal coxa saltans (snapping hip) occurs when the iliopsoas tendon snaps over the iliopectineal eminence. Can progress to iliopsoas bursitis. Pain is anterior groin, worsened by active hip flexion against resistance.

3. Sports Hernia and Groin Strain
Repetitive kicking or sprinting can overload the rectus abdominis-adductor aponeurosis at the pubic symphysis.

Evidence-Based Treatment Approaches
-------------------------------------
Stretching:
- Kneeling hip flexor stretch (Thomas stretch position): lengthens iliopsoas and rectus femoris
- 90-90 hip flexor stretch: targets psoas with lumbar neutral
- Psoas release in supine: passive hip extension over bolster

Strengthening:
- Seated march (isometric hip flexion): early psoas activation
- Hanging knee raise: eccentric loading through full hip flexion range
- Copenhagen adduction exercise: hip flexor and adductor co-contraction

Postural Correction:
- Posterior pelvic tilt exercises: reduce anterior pelvic tilt
- Glute strengthening (bridge, deadlift): reciprocal inhibition of hip flexors

CHAPTER 10: ILIOTIBIAL BAND (IT BAND)
======================================================================

Anatomy Overview
----------------
The iliotibial band (ITB) is a thickened lateral retinacular condensation of the tensor fascia latae and gluteus maximus. It inserts onto Gerdy's tubercle of the lateral tibia and the fibular head. The ITB does not stretch significantly; treatment targets the tensor fascia latae (TFL) and gluteus medius, which control ITB tension.

Common Pain Mechanisms
-----------------------
1. IT Band Syndrome (Friction Syndrome)
Repetitive knee flexion-extension cycling causes the ITB to impinge on the lateral femoral epicondyle at approximately 30° of knee flexion (the impingement zone). Highly prevalent in long-distance runners. Pain is focal at the lateral femoral epicondyle, worsened by downhill running and stair descent.

2. TFL and Gluteus Medius Tightness
Tight TFL increases ITB tension directly. Weak gluteus medius allows contralateral pelvic drop, increasing hip adduction and ITB strain during stance.

Red Flags (Differentiation)
-----------------------------
Distinguish from lateral collateral ligament sprain (Valgus/varus stress test), proximal tibiofibular joint dysfunction, and biceps femoris tendinopathy.

Evidence-Based Treatment Approaches
-------------------------------------
Load Management:
- Reduce training volume by 30-50% in the acute phase
- Avoid running downhill, stairs, and cycling until pain-free at 30° knee flexion

Soft Tissue Mobilisation:
- Foam rolling TFL: positions of sustained pressure over the lateral thigh proximal two-thirds; avoid rolling directly over the lateral epicondyle
- TFL stretch: lateral trunk lean with weight-bearing leg in adduction

Strengthening:
- Gluteus medius strengthening: clamshell, side-lying hip abduction, lateral band walks
- Resisted hip abduction in standing: functional closed-chain gluteus medius loading
- Single-leg squat with valgus control: trains gluteus medius to prevent pelvic drop

Return to Running:
- Pain-free single-leg squat at 30° knee flexion before resuming running
- Run-walk protocol: 1 minute running intervals, progressing by 10% weekly
